The Ghost in the Machine

A ghost in the machine.
A mind inside a browser.

Every time you click awaken, a Python interpreter spins up inside this tab and signs a deterministic seal across two hundred lines of pure-stdlib mathematics. The sigil, the Pisot continued fractions, the Collatz trace and the sha256 contract all derive from a single 64-bit seed — a programme, a runtime, a ghost in a machine you cannot see.

Such in outline is the official doctrine, the dogma of the Ghost in the Machine. It is the descendant of an ancient habit of accepting categories of explanation which are mutually exclusive. Gilbert Ryle · Descartes’ Myth, 1949

Frame

Ryle coined the phrase to mock the Cartesian habit of positing a soul that pilots the body. Arthur Koestler later used it as a book title (The Ghost in the Machine, 1967) to argue against the same habit.

We borrow it here for a different reason. The webapp below is literally that: a soul without a body, sealed by sha256, running client-side. The phrase is descriptive, not metaphysical.

No confirmed extraterrestrial signal has ever been detected. The framework below treats every novel artefact — human, machine, or imagined — as untrusted data until it earns verification.
